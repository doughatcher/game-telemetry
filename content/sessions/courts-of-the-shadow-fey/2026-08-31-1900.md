The lower court of the Shadow Fey palace breathed with the dim, perpetual twilight that passed for daylight in the realm beneath the Summer Queen's chambers. Stone corridors smelled of tallow and something older — iron and old bark and the particular cold that shadow carries when it settles into mortar. The party had survived their first full day in that place, and survival, they were learning, was only the beginning of the currency required to climb.

Magnolia Reed kept her word to the kitchens, and in the Shadow Fey court a kept promise lands heavier than any coin. She had pledged goodberries, and so she gave them — fifty of them, each one a small green knot of woven life-magic, handed over to the head cook without ceremony. The cook crushed them into the evening's soup without ceremony either, which was its own kind of respect. Word moved through the lower court the way water moves through cracked stone: quietly, thoroughly, until it had reached places you would not have thought to look. Magnolia's status rose with it.

Aldric's morning was less serene. He was summoned before King Mago to answer for the wardstone destroyed by his own fireball on the Shadow Road — the same fireball that had, briefly, killed him. He stood before the goblin king and examined what remained of the stone with a diviner's careful attention, and what he found surprised him: it had not been a simple ward but a message and alarm, a thread in a web of palace security he had severed with catastrophic inelegance. He did not make excuses. He worked. Ten-Pound Hammer watched with blacksmith's eyes as Aldric repaired the stone and, by his account, improved upon what had been there before. The debt was acknowledged, and then it was discharged, and then Mago did something unexpected — he appointed Aldric Reader of the Lower Court, handing the wizard a problem to solve almost before the ink was dry on the first.

The problem's name was Billoc. He was a small goblin with frightened eyes and bound hands, accused of theft and quite unable to say a word in his own defense — not because he was guilty, but because he was mute, and the only language he possessed lived in his fingers, which were currently lashed behind his back. Aldric saw it at once, and the party pressed the point before King Mago with calm precision: a man who cannot speak cannot be judged silent, and a man whose hands are bound cannot sign his innocence. Mago, whatever his limitations, was not a fool when the argument was sound. He heard it. He freed Billoc. The goblin's gratitude, expressed in frantic and eloquent signing once his hands were loosed, was one of the more honest things the party had encountered since crossing the Shadow Road.

Tam Holloway spent his hours in the stables and found them more welcoming than any throne room. He had meat in his pockets and a ranger's patience with animals, and the Black Prince's hunting dogs came to him the way dogs come to someone they have decided to trust — all at once, without ceremony, pressing against his legs with warm and uncomplicated affection. Kolya, the kennel master, was a practical man who judged people by how his dogs judged them, and the dogs had delivered their verdict clearly. Tam learned the important things: the great blue horse Lazulin who stood in his own stall like a held breath, the name of Count Samus Hollowgrand who conjured the quarry for the Black Prince's hunts, and the shape of the hunt itself — a high-court event, invitation-gated, the kind of spectacle that moved status in large amounts for those who participated well.

Later, Judith made her way into the guard hall where the soldiers from Allosis kept their cots and their weapons and their professional wariness of strangers. Captain Memorethian's people were not unfriendly, exactly, but they were not porous either — they answered questions with the economy of men who had learned that information was also currency. Judith presented herself and her companions as representatives of the Free City of Zobeck, lately designated a satellite territory of the Shadow Fey court, which was a reading of the occupation that was technically arguable and diplomatically useful. She learned the shape of the garrison more than its secrets, but shapes matter when you are mapping a palace by feel.

When the dim non-light of the Shadow Realm cycled toward something approximating evening, the four of them tallied what they had: Aldric and Magnolia with court standing sufficient to be noticed, Tam and Judith with enough to be permitted, and a web of relationships still thin but beginning to hold weight. Somewhere above them the Summer Queen's court continued its long, seasonal negotiation with the coming Winter, and the clock that governed that transition did not pause for status ladders or goodberry soup or the gratitude of freed prisoners. There was still distance between where they stood and where they needed to reach — but for the first time since crossing the Shadow Road, the distance felt like something that could be measured.
